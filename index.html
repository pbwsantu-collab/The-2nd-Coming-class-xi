The Poem: The Second Coming (1919)

By William Butler Yeats

Turning and turning in the widening gyre
The falcon cannot hear the falconer;
Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;
Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,
The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere
The ceremony of innocence is drowned;
The best lack all conviction, while the worst
Are full of passionate intensity.
Surely some revelation is at hand;
Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi
Troubles my sight: somewhere in sands of the desert
A shape with lion body and the head of a man,
A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,
Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it
Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds.
The darkness drops again; but now I know
That twenty centuries of stony sleep
Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,
And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,
Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

Comprehensive Critical Analysis

1. Historical Context & Background

Composed in January 1919 immediately following the horrors of World War I, W. B. Yeats wrote "The Second Coming" during a period of global devastation, the 1918 Influenza Pandemic, the 1917 Russian Bolshevik Revolution, and the escalating Anglo-Irish Conflict (Irish War of Independence). Yeats felt that Western Christian civilization had reached an irreparable crisis point and was collapsing under the weight of unprecedented violence and social upheaval.

2. Philosophical Context: Yeats's Theory of Historical Gyres

Central to understanding the poem is Yeats's esoteric mystical system described in his work A Vision. Yeats believed history moves not linearly, but in expanding and contracting circular spirals called gyres. Each historical cycle lasts roughly 2,000 years:

  • Ancient Pagan Era (2000 BCE – 0 CE): Initiated by the mythic union of Zeus and Leda (represented by the Swan).
  • Christian Era (0 CE – 1900s): Initiated by the birth of Jesus Christ in Bethlehem.
  • The Incoming Era (post-1900s): Reaching the maximum expansion of the Christian gyre where "the centre cannot hold," giving birth to a grotesque, totalitarian new era embodied by the rough beast.

3. Detailed Stanza-by-Stanza Breakdown

Stanza 1: Civilizational Breakdown & Disintegration (Lines 1–8)
The poem begins with the vivid metaphor of a falcon flying beyond the range of its trainer. As the spiral broadens, communication breaks down—symbolizing humanity's loss of connection with moral, spiritual, and social roots. The famous line "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold" conveys total political and social fragmentation. Traditional decencies (ceremony of innocence) are submerged in bloodshed (blood-dimmed tide). Yeats highlights a crucial political tragedy: wise and moral people (the best) are paralyzed by doubt, while ruthless extremists (the worst) rule with violent zeal.

Stanza 2: The Vision of the Beast & Apocalyptic Inversion (Lines 9–22)
The speaker senses that this chaos must herald a major cosmic transition—a Second Coming. However, instead of the peaceful return of Christ, the poet experiences a haunting vision drawn from Spiritus Mundi (the collective subconscious). In the desert, a Sphinx-like monster—part lion, part man—awakens. Its gaze is "blank and pitiless as the sun," utterly devoid of human empathy. After two millennia of "stony sleep," this terrifying creature is slouching towards Bethlehem to replace the Christian era with an age of tyrannical, raw force.

4. Core Themes

  • Collapse of Civilizational Order: Society's moral institutions lose their binding power when authority breaks down.
  • Inversion of Prophecy: Subversion of Christian biblical hope; salvation is replaced by an apocalyptic birth of chaos.
  • Passivity of the Wise vs. Zeal of Fanatics: The moral crisis where rational leadership abdicates responsibility to aggressive extremists.
  • Cyclical History: History is an unstoppable, alternating momentum of creation and destruction.

5. Poetic Style, Structure & Literary Devices

  • Form: Written in two stanzas of irregular blank verse (rough iambic pentameter without a fixed rhyme scheme), mirroring the decay of traditional order.
  • Simile: "A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun"—underscores absolute indifference.
  • Metaphor: The falcon and falconer; the blood-dimmed tide; the rocking cradle.
  • Rhetorical Question: The poem ends on a haunting question that leaves the reader in suspense and dread regarding humanity's impending future.
